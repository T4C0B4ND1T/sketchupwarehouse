Learn SketchUp first if you design buildings, interiors, furniture or landscapes and need to get from idea to a shareable model fast. Learn Rhino first if your shapes are curved, precise or generated by rules: product design, jewelry, boat hulls, facades with complex geometry. Plenty of designers end up using both, and the handoff between them is the part worth planning.

## The core difference: polygons vs NURBS

SketchUp is a direct modeler built on edges and faces. You draw a shape, pull it into volume and keep editing. It's quick because there is very little setup between you and the model. If the Push/Pull and Follow Me workflow is new to you, our guide to the [SketchUp Follow Me tool](/blog/sketchup-follow-me-tool/) shows how far that approach goes for moldings, pipes and lathed forms.

Rhino is a NURBS modeler. Curves and surfaces are defined mathematically, so a curve stays a true curve at any zoom, and you can build smooth, continuous surfaces and solids. That matters when a part has to be manufactured, 3D printed or milled with tight tolerances. Rhino can also work with meshes, but its strength is precise freeform geometry.

The practical result:

- **SketchUp** is fast for orthogonal and moderately curved forms. Curves are approximated by short straight segments, so very smooth organic surfaces are awkward.
- **Rhino** handles complex curvature well, but expect more commands, more settings and more decisions per model.

## Side-by-side comparison

| | SketchUp | Rhino |
|---|---|---|
| Modeling approach | Direct, edge-and-face | NURBS surfaces and solids (plus meshes) |
| Best for | Architecture, interiors, woodworking, landscape, concept massing | Product design, jewelry, marine, fabrication, complex facades |
| Learning curve | Gentle; productive in days | Steeper; command-driven |
| Parametric design | Via extensions | Grasshopper included with Rhino 8 |
| 2D documentation | LayOut (Pro and Studio plans) | Layouts inside Rhino; less drafting-oriented |
| Pricing model | Annual subscription | Perpetual license |
| Extension ecosystem | Extension Warehouse, large component library | Plugins, plus Grasshopper add-ons |

## Pricing: subscription vs perpetual

Check both vendors' pages before you buy, because prices change and vary by region. At the time of writing:

- **SketchUp** is sold by subscription. Trimble's plans page lists Go at $10.75 per month, Pro at $33.25 per month and Studio at $68.25 per month, all billed annually. That works out to $129, $399 and $819 per year. Pro is the plan most desktop users want: it adds the desktop modeler, LayOut and extension access.
- **Rhino 8** is a perpetual license. McNeel's store lists $995 for a single-user commercial license and $595 to upgrade from a previous version. Education pricing is much lower, at $195 for a full single-user license.

Over three years, SketchUp Pro costs about $1,200 against Rhino's $995 one-time. After that, Rhino is cheaper to keep running unless you pay for upgrades. SketchUp is cheaper to start, and cheaper if you only need it for a short project. Neither price includes a renderer, so budget for that separately; see [Lumion vs Enscape for SketchUp users](/blog/lumion-vs-enscape-for-sketchup-users/) if rendering is the next cost on your list.

## Grasshopper: the real reason many people pick Rhino

Grasshopper is Rhino's visual programming environment, and it ships with Rhino 8. You wire components together instead of writing code, and the model updates when you change an input. Think of a facade panel pattern, a gridshell or a parametric chair: change a slider, and 400 parts regenerate.

SketchUp has no built-in equivalent. You can get partway with extensions and the Ruby API, but if rule-driven geometry is central to your work, Rhino plus Grasshopper is the more natural environment. If it isn't, you can skip this entirely and lose nothing.

## Learning curve: what to expect

SketchUp's [first ten tools](/blog/sketchup-for-beginners/) are enough to model a room, a shed or a bookcase in your first sessions. The risk is bad habits: untidy geometry, no components, no tags. Fixing those is a discipline issue, not a learning wall.

Rhino takes longer to feel comfortable. You will learn about curve degree, surface continuity, the difference between a polysurface and a solid, and why a model fails to join. None of it is impossible, but expect a few weeks before it feels fluent. The reward is that you can build things SketchUp can't represent cleanly.

## Which industries lean which way

- **Architecture and interior design:** SketchUp dominates for early design, space planning and client communication. Rhino shows up when a firm designs complex forms or runs computational design.
- **Woodworking and cabinetry:** SketchUp. Simple solid geometry, quick dimensions and cut-list workflows fit it well.
- **Landscape:** SketchUp, usually with its terrain tools. See [SketchUp Sandbox terrain](/blog/sketchup-sandbox-terrain/) for the basics.
- **Product, jewelry, marine and fabrication:** Rhino, because of surface quality and precision.

## Walkthrough: a pavilion with a curved canopy

Here's a realistic deliverable that uses both tools: a small garden pavilion with a double-curved roof, and a set of client drawings.

1. **Block out in SketchUp.** Model the site, the posts, the floor and a simple roof volume. Use components for repeated posts so edits propagate.
2. **Export the roof footprint.** From SketchUp Pro, use File > Export > 3D Model and choose DWG, OBJ or FBX. SketchUp's Help Center notes that CAD (DWG/DXF) and FBX export are available on the Pro and Go plans.
3. **Build the roof in Rhino.** Open or import the file, draw guide curves for the canopy edge, and create a lofted surface. If you want rule-based rafters, build them in Grasshopper.
4. **Bring it back as a mesh.** Export the roof from Rhino as OBJ or FBX, or save it as a SketchUp file, and import it into SketchUp. Group or componentize it right away so it doesn't merge with your other geometry.
5. **Document in LayOut.** Place scenes, add dimensions and issue the drawing set.

Keep the master model in one place. Rhino-made surfaces are meshes once they're in SketchUp, so edit the roof in Rhino and re-import rather than patching faces by hand.

## Moving files between SketchUp and Rhino

Rhino reads and writes SketchUp files, but with a version limit. McNeel's Rhino 8 help says Rhino reads and writes SketchUp file formats up to 2021. If you work in a newer SketchUp, don't rely on direct .skp import for current files. Use an exchange format instead:

- **DWG/DXF** for lines, profiles and simple geometry.
- **OBJ or FBX** for meshes and textured models.

Rhino's import dialog lets you choose how faces arrive (planar trimmed surfaces or meshes), whether to import curves, whether to join and weld, and whether groups map to layers. The Rhino help also warns that Rhino may crash if the machine runs out of memory on a SketchUp file with too many meshes. So clean the SketchUp file first: purge unused items, explode nothing you don't need to, and import a stripped copy.

### Worth it?

- **Time cost:** a few weeks to learn Rhino basics if you already know SketchUp; longer for Grasshopper.
- **Money:** $995 for a commercial Rhino 8 license, on top of your SketchUp subscription. Education pricing is far lower.
- **Skip it if:** you model buildings, furniture or landscapes with straight and simply curved geometry and never hand off to fabrication or parametric workflows. SketchUp alone covers you.

## Which should you learn?

- **New to 3D, working in architecture, interiors or woodworking:** SketchUp, then add Rhino only if you hit a wall.
- **Heading into product design, jewelry or computational design:** Rhino first.
- **Already know SketchUp and want smoother curves or parametric control:** add Rhino and Grasshopper and keep SketchUp for layout and quick massing.

## FAQ

### Is Rhino harder than SketchUp?
Yes, for most people. SketchUp's direct modeling is easier to pick up, while Rhino asks you to understand curves, surfaces and solids. Rhino's payoff is precision and control over complex shapes.

### Can Rhino open SketchUp files?
Yes. McNeel's Rhino 8 help says Rhino reads and writes SketchUp files up to the 2021 version. For newer SketchUp files, export to DWG, OBJ or FBX instead.

### Does SketchUp have anything like Grasshopper?
Not built in. Extensions and the Ruby API can automate parts of a workflow, but Grasshopper, which comes with Rhino 8, is a dedicated visual programming environment.

### Is Rhino cheaper than SketchUp?
It depends on the time horizon. Rhino is a one-time $995 commercial license; SketchUp Pro is $399 per year. Over about three years they cost similar amounts, and after that Rhino is cheaper to keep. Check both vendors' pages for current pricing in your region.
